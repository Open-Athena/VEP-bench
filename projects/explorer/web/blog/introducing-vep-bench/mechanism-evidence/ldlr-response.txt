## Basis of the predictions

These are **sequence-based point predictions, not measured effects**. I assign the largest negative effects to disruption of the clearest activator motif, intermediate effects to plausible promoter-binding elements, and values close to zero where the sequence does not support a reliable mechanism. The small numerical differences among low-confidence predictions should not be interpreted as precisely resolved affinity differences.

All positions below refer to the **unmodified, 318-bp insert**. The deletion alleles retain their VCF anchor bases:

- **V19:** deletes **C128**.
- **V43:** deletes **G243**.
- **V44:** deletes **G265**.

The absence of a separate minimal promoter matters: this insert must itself support transcription initiation. Consequently, mutations affecting promoter-factor recruitment could reduce both basal transcription and activation. Conversely, without a mapped transcription start site, it would be unsafe to label every downstream base as either promoter DNA or transcribed 5′ UTR.

The most informative sequence feature is the exact **sterol-response element `ATCACCCCAC` at 161–170**. Nearby C/T-rich elements provide a plausible cooperative promoter architecture. HepG2 cells make SREBP- and Sp-family mechanisms biologically credible, although the assay description does not establish nuclear SREBP abundance or activation by lipid depletion.

## 1. Canonical sterol-response element: strongest predicted losses

The sequence at **161–170, `ATCACCCCAC`**, is a well-established SREBP-recognition sequence. Its two `CAC` segments, at **163–165** and **168–170**, make the substitutions at 164, 165, and 169 especially concerning.

Disrupting this element is expected to reduce activator occupancy and its cooperation with neighboring promoter-bound factors. These are my most confident directional predictions, although their magnitudes depend on basal SREBP activity in the particular HepG2 culture.

## 2. Flanking C/T-rich promoter elements

Two related sequences flank the SRE:

- **126–136:** `CTCCTCCTCTT`
- **177–187:** `CTCCTCCCCCT`

Their reverse complements are purine-rich, and the second contains a particularly conspicuous **C run at 182–186**. These are compatible with noncanonical Sp-family/pyrimidine-box recognition. They are **not both textbook `GGGCGG` GC boxes**, so identifying the bound protein specifically as SP1 would require experimental evidence.

My working model is that these elements recruit activating promoter factors and cooperate with the SRE-bound factor. Under that model, interrupting their sequence organization is usually detrimental.

## 3. Existing ETS-like sites and the G243 deletion

### V06: loss of an existing core

**V06, G74C: −0.45.**

The reference contains **`GGAA` at 74–77**. G74C changes it to `CGAA`, eliminating that ETS core. ETS-family activators, including broadly expressed promoter-associated ETS proteins, provide a plausible mechanism for reduced reporter activity.

The core disruption is unambiguous; occupancy and functional importance are not. This is why its predicted loss is smaller than the losses assigned to the exact SRE.

### V43: improved flank without deletion of the core

**V43, del G243: +0.35.**

The reference sequence at **241–249** is:

```text
GCGGGAAGC
```

Deleting G243 gives:

```text
GCGGAAGC
```

The existing **`GGAA` at reference positions 244–247 remains intact**. The deletion instead places C immediately before it, producing the extended `CGGAAG` sequence characteristic of many ETS-binding sites. This is a more persuasive potential affinity improvement than creation of an isolated four-base core.

The opposite-strand ETS core **`TTCC` at 256–259** also remains intact. Because the deletion is upstream of both cores, their mutual spacing is unchanged.

The positive prediction remains conditional: binding measurements would be needed to establish that the altered flank actually improves occupancy in this construct.

## 4. Weak nuclear-receptor-like sequences

Several sequences resemble nuclear-receptor half-sites, but none warrants a strong factor assignment:

- **104–109:** `ATGTCA`, a near match to `AGGTCA`.
- **143–148:** `AGGTGA`, another near match.
- **264–279:** `AGGACA-CAGC-AGGTCG`, two imperfect half-sites separated by four bases.

The last arrangement is compatible in spacing with some nuclear-receptor dimers, but both half-sites are imperfect. It is not evidence by itself for an occupied LXR/RXR site, and it is not a canonical HNF4-style DR1 arrangement. I use these observations only for weak directional priors.

There is also an important alternative interpretation around V13–V16: **104–106 is an `ATG`**. The supplied construct information does not establish that, and a translation effect would not automatically produce the same change in barcode-linked RNA output. I therefore do **not** treat these variants as established upstream-ORF mutations.

## Uncertainty and evidence needed

The strongest conclusion is the expected negative direction for mutations disrupting **161–170**. The main uncertainties are:

1. **Actual factor activity:** untreated HepG2 culture conditions do not establish how much active nuclear SREBP is present.
2. **Occupancy and factor identity:** the C-rich, ETS-like, and receptor-like sites require binding or factor-perturbation evidence.
3. **Competition and cooperation:** a newly created ETS site could enhance transcription, compete with another activator, or recruit a different ETS-family member.
4. **Transcription-start placement:** TSS mapping is needed before assigning initiator, 5′-UTR, or upstream-ORF mechanisms.
5. **Magnitude:** the numerical coefficients require the reporter measurements themselves for calibration.

Thus, the large SRE-disrupting losses are more defensible than the small peripheral effects, and the weak gain-of-site predictions are particularly liable to change sign.
